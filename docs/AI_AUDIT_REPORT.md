# AI Audit Report: .ntu Domain Registrar

## Executive summary

A sealed bid ntu domain registrar in solidity and also a React app that connects to Metamask was built by us. The flow is as such, a bidder would first submit a hidden commitment of a fixed 0.1 ETH deposit and during the reveal phase they would disclose the name, bid and salt. The highest valid bid then wins and an earlier commitment wins a tie and after finalization, users can then resolve the name to an address and send ETH to the address.

We used the help of AI assistance like ChatGPT and Codex to turn the brief into checks, compare contract and frontend code and also work through bugs. Whenever a change had been suggested, until relevant tests or browser flow had been done, we did not treat a generated fix as completed. Our most serious problems identified were in the wallet experience, a few of them are recovering a bid after a reload, keeping the right wallet and network context, showing the complete payment recipient and displaying auction deadlines correctly. One important issue was bid recovery and we learnt if a user lost their saved bid details after deposit had been paid, they might not be able to reveal their bid after and this is even when the smart contract is working correctly.

In the end our final local suite passed **182 named tests which is 153 Solidity and 29 Mocha**. We also built and linted the frontend, deployed and verified a fresh local registrar, checked recovery and payment in a browser, and verified the recorded Sepolia deployment, so this report explains what each check showed and where our evidence is limited.

## 1. Initial Specification & System Scope

So our starting goal taken from Option 3 of the project brief, was an Ethereum testnet registrar for .ntu names. A user should be able to search for a name and also bid without revealing the name or price during commit, reveal later, register the winner, resolve the name to an address and also pay that address through the web app. We chose short phases so the entire auction could be demonstrated, then developed docs/SPEC.md with contract methods, phase rules, accounting, backup format and 29 acceptance checks, then the working specification was refined during implementation and AI-assisted review.

| **Design decision**   | **Implemented rule**                                                                                                                                               | **Reason it matters**                                                                                                                    |
|-----------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Hidden commitment     | The contract receives round ID, digest and a fixed deposit; the name, bid and salt are disclosed only at reveal.                                                   | Makes different bid amounts indistinguishable from the deposit alone and keeps the target name out of public commit calldata and events. |
| Round schedule        | Explicit start, then 120 seconds for commit and 120 seconds for reveal.                                                                                            | Contract block timestamps, not the browser clock, determine inclusion and validity.                                                      |
| Auction outcome       | Highest valid reveal wins; earlier on-chain commitment breaks an equal-bid tie.                                                                                    | Produces a deterministic result independent of reveal ordering.                                                                          |
| Deposit accounting    | Each commitment locks 0.1 ETH. A winning bidder recovers deposit minus its bid, a valid loser recovers the full deposit, and an unrevealed bidder loses 0.001 ETH. | Keeps refunds and treasury proceeds explicit and prevents the treasury from taking unsettled bidder escrow.                              |
| Ownership and payment | Finalization registers the winner; the owner may update a separate resolution address; a payment checks that address during the transaction.                       | Distinguishes property rights from payment destination and blocks a stale reviewed recipient.                                            |
| Recovery              | A versioned local preimage records chain, registrar, wallet, round, exact wei amount, label, digest and salt before wallet approval.                               | A bidder needs the same preimage to reveal; losing it after paying the deposit could cause a non-reveal penalty.                         |

The contract decides ownership, reservations, deadlines, results and withdrawal credits, then the frontend prepares the secret commitment details and also stores a recovery copy which then would ask MetaMask to approve transactions. Moving on, browser storage helps a user reveal after a reload, but the contract remains the source of truth, then we also allowed different names to share a round and a later round to begin while an older name still awaited finalization.

Our security checks covered many things like invalid names and amounts, copied commitments, exact deadlines, duplicate actions, unauthorized resolution changes, rejected ETH transfers, reentrancy, changing payment recipients and backup integrity. However, expiry, renewal, subdomains, resale and ownership transfer were outside and not in the base scope, to add on, reverse resolution was a bonus feature.

## 2. Development Methodology & AI Tooling Infrastructure

Regarding Methodology and AI tooling infrastructure, our project used Solidity and Hardhat 3 for the registrar and tests, a compiler-exported ABI for the frontend interface, and React, Vite, ethers v6 and MetaMask for the application. The team responsibilities on our project were split between the contract, the wallet-connected frontend, and deployment/independent validation. Then, we brought all those branches together only after checking that the ABI, chain settings and user flows are on the same page. Browser feedback on backups, payments and the phase timer shaped the later frontend fixes.

For the work recorded here and ai tools we used, we used ChatGPT/Codex to review the specification and code, compare frontend revisions with the contract ABI, suggest focused tests, diagnose failures, prepare targeted corrections and help draft documentation. Our repeatable checks were repository scripts rather than a custom autonomous agent, team members ran the Windows MetaMask flows, reported what actually appeared, and repeated them after fixes. Decisions then, such as retaining a compatible dependency toolchain and separating browser evidence from contract tests were made after reviewing the results.

Our usual loop was to state the expected behaviour, inspect the relevant source and ABI, reproduce the problem, make a small change, and then rerun the appropriate test. For wallet issues, we also checked the live page, a simulated EVM test can check a contract call, but it cannot prove that MetaMask showed the right approval screen or that a downloaded backup survived a refresh.

## 4. AI-Generated Architecture

The initial system layout we reviewed was one registrar contract and a wallet-connected web app, without an application backend. Then the UI connected through MetaMask to a configured chain and contract address, the registrar kept round timing, commitments, per-name auctions, ownership and resolution, locked deposits and withdrawal credits. AI-assisted review was very useful and what we identified with it was that the first frontend path did not fully account for a rejected approval, a pending transaction, an imported backup or a changed payment recipient, however we addressed these issues. The final architecture was updated on those recovery and confirmation states without replacing the contract's auction model.

| **Boundary**                      | **Data flowing across it**                                                                                 | **Principal check**                                                                              |
|-----------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Browser to wallet                 | Proposed transaction and ETH value; locally generated preimage remains in browser backup.                  | User approval, selected account and selected chain.                                              |
| Wallet to registrar               | A commit digest and fixed deposit, then a reveal with label, amount and salt.                              | Contract validates round phase, bidder, amount, digest and reservation.                          |
| Registrar to wallet/receiver      | Settlement creates credit; withdraw transfers caller credit; name payment forwards only that call's value. | Credit is cleared before transfer; payment checks current resolution against reviewed recipient. |
| Source checkout to deployed chain | ABI, bytecode and recorded compiler input.                                                                 | Deployment verifier checks network, transaction, runtime code, treasury and ABI.                 |

For a canonical label L, integer bid B and fresh random 32-byte salt S, the browser computes:

H = keccak256(abi.encode(chainId, registrarAddress, roundId, bidderAddress, L, B, S)).

These seven fields and standard ABI encoding bind the hidden bid to a wallet, round, chain and registrar. This makes it so that someone cannot copy the digest and reveal it from a different wallet, the commit transaction exposes the round, digest and fixed deposit, but not the label, amount or salt. The wallet's participation and timing are still visible, and the reveal makes the name and bid public later.

The flow is start round, commit, reveal, finalize each name, settle each bid, and withdraw any credit. In our architecture, a valid bidder settles after finalization and an unrevealed bidder can settle after the round ends, then from the 0.1 ETH deposit D, a winner receives D minus its bid B, a valid loser receives all of D, and a non-revealing bidder receives D minus the 0.001 ETH penalty. The treasury receives the winning price and any penalty through the contract's accounting.

Lastly, finalization makes the winner the owner and initially resolves the name to that wallet. Then after, the owner can later choose a different nonzero receiving address. For a payment, the app shows the resolved address and sends it as the expected recipient to sendToDomain. The contract also checks the current record again before forwarding ETH, so a changed recipient cannot silently replace the one the sender reviewed.

## 5. Problems Discovered & Vulnerability Analysis

Our most important findings were in the frontend's connection to the contract and wallet, we did not reproduce a new Solidity logic vulnerability and the contract tests cover authorization, reentrancy, accounting and deadlines. However, that does not prove the contract is confirmed to be free of bugs, so for each issue, we explain the behaviour, why the code or workflow appears to have missed it, what we changed and how we checked the fix.

### FE-07: Reveal backup and pending receipt recovery

**What went wrong and why it mattered.** In an early browser check, importing a settled bid lost its on-chain commitment ID and original time. The older frontend also saved a bid only after the commit call finished. If someone paid the fixed deposit and then closed the page before the preimage was saved, they could lose the name, amount or salt needed to reveal and incur the non-reveal penalty. During a separate check, a pending record survived a refresh but remained labelled Submitted after MetaMask reported a failure.

**Why we think it happened.** The original flow concentrated on a successful transaction and It did not properly treat preparation, wallet approval, broadcast, receipt and chain confirmation as separate steps, this means that things like bid details were not always saved before interruptions occur and the user could lose information that is needed for the reveal phase.

**What we changed and checked.** The app now saves and offers a backup before asking for webpage and MetaMask approval. Records are separated by chain, registrar, wallet and round. Import checks fields, the exact wei amount and the commitment digest; receipt and chain checks restore the on-chain ID without discarding the secret. We restored earlier #1/#2 IDs and times, preserved the exact 30000000000000001 wei amount for #7, and rejected modified or wrong-context backups without replacing valid records. Canceling confirmation left a Prepared, not submitted record after refresh. On the final combined frontend, a pending bid survived reload, became confirmed ID #1 after mining, and retained its original time; a backup later restored its settled and revealed state. The current-source EVM test separately checked a failed receipt and an RPC interruption after broadcast that retained the transaction hash.

### FE-06: Payment confirmation and a changing recipient

**What went wrong and why it mattered.** An older frontend path sent ETH directly to an address read earlier from the name. If the owner changed that address in between, the sender could pay a destination they had not just reviewed. The later contract method could reject a changed recipient, but the interface still lacked a clear screen showing the complete recipient, amount and network before approval.

**Why we think it happened.** Even though the contract has checks for the payment recipient, our frontend didn’t display clearly on the entire receiving address and payment details before user confirms the transaction.

**What we changed and checked.** The app now shows the full address, amount and chain, then calls sendToDomain(label, expectedRecipient). In the EVM test, changing resolution after preview rejected the stale payment; a fresh preview succeeded. We repeated that race in the browser by changing the resolution in another tab. The old review was rejected, the next review reported a successful 0.001 ETH payment.

**FE-09 and FE-10: Network context and visible input validation**

**What went wrong.** When MetaMask was on Sepolia but the site pointed at the local registrar, a read returned an opaque BAD_DATA error. At another point the navbar could call a local connection Sepolia. The old name field also silently stripped an underscore, making the submitted label differ from the text a user typed.

**Why we think it happened and how we fixed it.** The frontend was not properly checked on whether MetaMask was actually connected to the same network as the configured smart contract and also automatically modified domain names that were invalid instead of showing users what exactly was wrong with their input.

We added explicit chain and deployed-code checks, made the navbar show the active network, and kept invalid text visible so Search could explain the problem. With wallet chain 11155111 and registrar chain 31337, the browser showed the mismatch and blocked search and write actions. Typing bad_name remained visible and produced a syntax message. Contract-side label validation remains an independent check.

### FE-11: Countdown disagreed with blockchain phase

**What went wrong.** We saw the page claim 7m54s remained in a reveal period that lasts only 120 seconds. It offered Reveal when the contract would reject it. Our first clock correction uncovered a second case: the page reached zero while the latest mined block was still five seconds before the on-chain deadline.

**Why we think it happened and how we fixed it.** The browser's clock and a local test chain's block timestamp can diverge, so we changed the countdown to use chain time as its anchor and to show Awaiting chain block when the estimate has expired but no block has confirmed the next phase. In the browser it moved to Round ended when a new block crossed the deadline. A reveal included one second before its on-chain deadline succeeded with receipt status 1. So, this showed that the transaction’s block timestamp would determine if a reveal is accepted and not the countdown that was displayed on the webpage.

**FE-01 and historical interface defects**

The production build initially failed because unused frontend paths contained unresolved imports. Removing them let the TypeScript/Vite build pass. We also reproduced five problems in the captured older handler, a two-character label was accepted, round zero was queried before a round existed, an older reservation was misclassified, a resolution address was shown as the owner, and a payment bypassed the checked contract path. Several had already been corrected in a newer frontend revision. We therefore kept that reproduction as a record of what we found, but tested the delivered handler separately instead of reporting old behaviour as a current bug.

### DEPLOY-02: Compiler metadata changed across checkouts

The Sepolia deployment was compiled from source with Windows CRLF line endings. Rebuilding the same source from a Linux LF checkout changed the creation-data hash because the compiler metadata changed. That could make verification appear to fail even though the deployed contract was the same. We recorded the original input format and replayed it with the pinned Solidity 0.8.34 compiler and forge-std remapping. The offline LF-checkout test reproduced the recorded creation hash, including constructor arguments. Windows verification against Sepolia separately returned verified true. We did not capture a public-RPC verifier run from the LF checkout.

### DEP-01: Development-tool dependency advisories

Our first contract-tooling audit showed 22 affected package entries. We applied a compatible update and reran validation. The 1 November snapshot then showed 13 high and 5 moderate entries, mostly in transitive development tooling. A later Windows install reported 13 high and 3 moderate in contracts and 10 moderate in frontend dependencies, but these are different audit dates and package trees, not one combined count.

We did not claim the remaining advisories were fixed, the suggested direct fixes included major or incompatible Hardhat plugin and Mocha changes, so a forced upgrade would have required another migration and full validation pass. The report records that limitation and an affected package entry is not itself proof of an exploitable flaw in the deployed registrar.

## 7. Improvements & Refinements

We replaced a handwritten interface assumption with an ABI exported from the compiler, deployment records now connect the chain, address, treasury, source, ABI, runtime code and transaction. The fresh-checkout exercise and CRLF compiler replay made it possible to verify that connection on another machine instead of assuming that a local build matched the Sepolia deployment.

The bid flow now treats preparation, submission, confirmation and failure as different states. A user can save the preimage before approving; a broadcast record keeps its hash while waiting for a receipt; a confirmed record gains an on-chain ID. Import checks the exact amount and wallet/chain context before merging it into existing history. This was more work than a single optimistic commit successful message, but it addressed the failure we actually saw after reload.

For payments, we added a clear review step and bound the displayed recipient to the contract call. For deadlines, we let the blockchain phase decide what the app can offer and treat the visible timer as an estimate. We also changed the routine frontend regression check to run on the current handler while retaining the old reproduction for historical comparison. None of these integration repairs required rewriting the original Solidity auction rules.

## 8. Lessons Learned

**Where AI helped.** ChatGPT/Codex was effective when we gave it a small, checkable task and the relevant contract, ABI and failure output. So, it helped us turn the broad registrar brief into concrete cases, compare old and current frontend behaviour, propose an exact-wei test and reason about backup and deployment verification. This saved time in finding places to inspect and the useful output was usually a hypothesis or a narrow patch, followed by a test we could run ourselves.

**Where it fell short.** Code review alone did not catch the full wallet experience and problems were not very obvious when reviewing code alone, we discovered lost backup metadata, a canceled approval that still needed a saved preimage, a wrong-network message and a stale payment review by using the browser. AI-assisted analysis also had to be kept tied to a source revision, a defect reproduced on an archived handler was not necessarily still present in the current app. Lastly, the clock issue was another reminder that an apparently reasonable frontend calculation can be wrong when the local EVM and computer clocks diverge.

**How we kept control of the result.** We checked proposed fixes against the compiled ABI, reran targeted and full tests, examined transaction receipts where available, and then repeated the wallet flow, the most important example was bid recovery, a passing hash test did not tell us whether a user could reveal after closing the page. Only a prepared backup, reload, pending receipt and restored record demonstrated that path. We also kept testnet verification separate from the local browser evidence instead of treating either as proof of the other.

**What we would change next time.** What we would change the next time is that we would design recovery before the first paid wallet action, test rejected approvals and reloads early, and require a visible full-recipient confirmation before payments. We would also record the chain ID, contract address and compiler input alongside every deployment check.
